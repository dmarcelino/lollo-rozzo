# Browser

Each stack can give its agents a real web browser: a Chromium they drive one step at a time, to do what reading a page can't, such as signing in to a site, filling a form or a cart, or getting an order ready. You watch it live on your own page, take over a tab when something needs you (a CAPTCHA, a code sent to your phone, a payment), and choose which sites stay signed in and where agents may finish a checkout. By default an agent's "Place order" waits for you: you place it with one press, place it yourself, or decline. It is opt-in, behind the `lollo-browser` profile, which needs the egress proxy (`lollo-pipelock`).

![You ask, your agent drives the browser, the vault approves the password, and a checkout comes back to you](diagrams/browser-flow.svg)

An interactive version, [browser-flow.html](diagrams/browser-flow.html), opens in a browser from a clone of this repo.

How the browser is built and wired, for developers: [browser/README.md](../browser/README.md).

## Using it
1. **Ask your agent.** "Order the printer ink I usually buy at shop.example.com", "fill in the delivery form on carrier.example.com and stop before paying". Agents use the browser only when reading a page is not enough: a site that needs its scripts, a sign-in, a form, a cart or an order. Each piece of work is a **task**, with a purpose the agent writes ("order printer ink at shop.example.com, as asked in the chat"), which you see on the page.
2. **Open `https://<your lollo>/browser/`** and sign in as usual; `<your lollo>` is your stack's lollo address, `<name>-lollo.<domain>`, and its home page links to it as **Browser**. The page has four sections, and a link to the lollo's **Notifications** page:
   - **Tasks:** under **Waiting for you**, the hand-offs, each with the agent's summary, the task, the site and the page it was on. Under **Open tasks**, each task with its tabs and their addresses: **Watch** a tab, **Take over** or **Give back**, **End task**, or **End all tasks**, which closes every tab at once.
   - **Live view:** the whole browser window, tabs and address bar included. Watching is read-only: "Your clicks and keys reach the browser only after you take over." **Check the address bar before you type anything or pay**: it shows the site you are really on.
   - **Sites:** for each site, **Keep me signed in** and **Agents may finish checkouts**, when a sign-in was last saved, **Forget**, and **Forget all sign-ins**. **Add a site** sets them up ahead of time (a site already listed is changed in its own row); a site is the whole registrable domain, so `shop.example.com` covers all of `example.com`.
   - **History:** what happened, newest first, filtered by task and site: tasks started and ended, pages opened, forms submitted, secrets typed (the site and the field, never the value), hand-offs, checkouts, refusals, and your own answers and settings. Addresses are shown without their query.
   - **Notifications:** turn them on once per device on the lollo's own Notifications page, `https://<your lollo>/notifications/`, which this link opens; the same switch covers the vault's notifications too ([notifications.md](notifications.md)). On a phone, first add Lollo to the Home Screen (on iPhone: Share, then Add to Home Screen) and open it from there.
3. **When an agent needs you**, it asks for a **hand-off**: a CAPTCHA or "verify you are human" page, a code sent to your phone or email, a sign-in it has no login for, a payment. A notification arrives and opens the hand-off's page, with the summary, the task, the site, the page it was on, and **Open the live view**, **Done** and **Decline**. The agent says in the chat what it asked, too. Open the live view, press **Take over**, do what is needed, press **Give back**, then **Done**; or **Decline**, and the agent stops that part and asks you in the chat how to go on. While you have control of a tab, the agents' actions on it are refused, and they can't close it or end its task, so you are never fighting over the mouse. You control one tab at a time, and the live view stays on it until you give it back; if a payment opens a window of its own, give the tab back and take over that window. The summary was written by an agent, which a page may have misled: read the address bar, not the summary, before you type anything.
4. **Checkouts.** Agents are told to order only what you asked for in the chat, and to tell you the shop, the items, the delivery and the total before the last step. On a site where **Agents may finish checkouts** is off, which is every site until you turn it on, the browser recognises the agent's "Place order" click and holds it: it becomes a hand-off, titled "A checkout for you". Check the order in the live view first, then on the hand-off's page:
   - **Place this order**: the browser presses the agent's button for you, once, while you watch. It does so only if the tab is still on the page it was held on and the button is still there; otherwise nothing is pressed and the page tells you to take over.
   - **Place it and always allow** the site: the same, and the site's **Agents may finish checkouts** is turned on, so later orders there go ahead without asking (turn it off on **Sites**).
   - Take over in the live view, place the order yourself, give the tab back and press **I placed it myself**.
   - **Decline**: nothing is ordered, and the agent asks you in the chat how to go on.

   You approve only on that page, never from the notification: the notification shows an agent's summary, and only the live view shows you the real page. On a site you allowed, the agent's click goes ahead and you get a notification ("Browser: Checkout submitted") that opens the task's history. Recognising a checkout is a guess: see [Security model](#security-model) for what it can miss and what does hold.
5. **Staying signed in.** Every task starts signed out of every site, and what it signs in to is gone when it ends, except on the sites you keep signed in on **Sites**. For those, the browser saves that site's cookies and storage when a task ends, and every new task starts with them. **Every agent of your stack can use a kept sign-in**, without asking you or the vault. **Forget** deletes a site's saved sign-in at once; a task already open keeps what it loaded until it ends. Agents can't change these settings, and they are told never to ask you to keep a sign-in.
6. **Seeing a page in the chat.** Ask "show me the front page of news.example.com" and the agent takes a screenshot of the tab and the chat shows it. Lettuce saves it in the agent's workspace under `mcp/` (or the agent saves it under `browser-screenshots/` when Lettuce could not pass the picture on), It is the visible part of the page, masked like every screenshot (password, card and code fields, and fields typed from the vault). The file stays in the agent's workspace, like its other files (in the code editor and in `backup`); delete it there when you no longer need it. Agents are told to take one this way only when you ask to see a page, and not of a page showing your details unless you asked for that page; screenshots they take for themselves go to a temporary folder inside the stack's container, outside the workspace, and are deleted. These are instructions to the agent, not something the browser enforces.
7. **Stopping it.** When an agent has done what you asked (an order placed, a form sent), it tells you what it did, without repeating your address, phone or email, and asks whether you need anything else on that site or whether it should close the browser; say so in the chat. **End task** or **End all tasks** closes the tabs and throws away what those tasks held (sign-ins, carts). Tasks also end on their own after 15 minutes without the agent doing anything in them (not while you have control of one of their tabs), after 4 hours, or when the browser restarts. A hand-off ends with its task: an agent waiting on one past that tells you it lapsed.

### Signing in with the vault
With the [vault](vault.md), an agent can sign in with one of your logins without the password entering the conversation. It reads the login's public username and websites, asks the vault for the password (and the authenticator key, if the site asks for a code) with a purpose naming the site and the task, and you approve it on the vault's page as usual. The value then goes straight into the field on that site: snapshots show the field as masked, and an authenticator code is computed in the browser, so neither the key nor the code comes back to the agent. If the page, or the frame holding the field, is not on the login's site over HTTPS, the browser types nothing and the agent stops and tells you the address it was on.

A delivery address, phone or email a form needs goes the same way: the agent asks the vault for those fields of your identity, with a purpose naming the shop and the order, and types them without seeing them; the browser then masks them in what it shows the agent, a confirmation page included, even written another way. Details your shop account already holds show on its pages, and the agent is told never to repeat them; that is an instruction, not something the browser can enforce.

Card fields often sit in a payment provider's frame, a frame from another site inside the shop's page. The browser types a vault card there only for a short list of well-known providers (Stripe, Adyen, Braintree, Checkout.com, Mollie, Square and PayPal), and only when the page around the frame is the shop's over HTTPS; **History** names the provider. With any other provider, the agent hands the payment to you. Without a vault login, or when you prefer, the agent hands the sign-in to you: sign in through the live view, and the agent continues in that task.

### What it can't do
- Download or upload files: a page's download fails and nothing is saved.
- Get around CAPTCHAs, "checking your browser" pages or other bot checks: those are handed to you, and some sites may refuse the browser altogether.
- Open `file:`, `chrome:`, `data:` and similar addresses, or reach addresses inside your server or network: only public websites, through the stack's proxy.
- Open your own Lettuce or lollo pages, any stack's on your server, or your Access sign-in page ([Security model](#security-model)).
- Remember anything between tasks but the sign-ins you keep: no saved passwords, cards or history.

## Running it
- **Turn it on.** The browser needs the egress proxy, so add both:
  ```bash
  lolloctl profiles <current list>,lollo-pipelock,lollo-browser   # e.g. cloudflared,search,lollo-pipelock,lollo-browser
  lolloctl mcp all                                                # adds the browser to each stack's Settings -> MCP
  lolloctl skills all                                             # installs the using-the-browser skill
  ```
  `profiles` refuses `lollo-browser` without `lollo-pipelock`. It routes `https://<lollo>/browser/` to the browser on every stack, on the stack's lollo address and its own Access application, and generates each stack's engine secret (`BROWSER_ENGINE_SECRET` in `stack.env`). The browser holds no push keys: it sends its notifications through the stack's notifications service, `lollo-notifications`, which runs whenever the browser or the vault is on, with a token of its own that `up` makes ([notifications.md](notifications.md)). `up` then starts two containers per stack: `lollo-browser-engine`, where Chromium loads pages, first and alone, and once it is healthy `lollo-browser`, which drives it and serves the page. The engine image, with Chromium and its fonts, is about 1 GB.
- **Register it with the agents.** `mcp` adds `{"name": "browser", "transport": "http", "url": "http://lollo-browser:8000/mcp"}` once per stack, so a person who removes it in Settings -> MCP keeps it removed (`mcp <name> --force` adds it again); a different server of theirs named `browser` is left alone. `add` does this for a new stack. Dropping the profile removes the route, the containers and the entry `mcp` registered. Registering it gives agents nothing they could not reach over the stack's network anyway; what limits them is on the person's page and in the vault.
- **Host requirements for Chromium's sandbox.** Chromium always runs with its own sandbox on, and the sandbox needs unprivileged user namespaces on the host's kernel:
  - `sysctl user.max_user_namespaces` above 0;
  - on Debian kernels, `sysctl kernel.unprivileged_userns_clone` not 0 (set it to 1);
  - on Ubuntu 23.10 and later, keep Docker's default AppArmor profile for the engine. With `apparmor=unconfined`, Ubuntu's restriction on unprivileged user namespaces breaks the sandbox, and `lolloctl check` refuses it anyway.

  Set a sysctl for good in a file under `/etc/sysctl.d/`. On macOS, these are the settings of Docker's Linux VM.
- **When the sandbox can't start**, `up` stops the engine and fails with `<name>: Chromium's sandbox cannot start on this host, so the browser does not run (it never runs without its sandbox)`, naming the settings above; the rest of that stack does not start. Fix the host and run `lolloctl up <name>`, or turn the `lollo-browser` profile off. Nothing ever starts Chromium without its sandbox. When the engine fails for another reason, `up` says "the browser's engine did not become healthy": read `lolloctl compose <name> logs lollo-browser-engine`.
- **Resource use.** The engine is capped at 2 GiB of memory (its shared memory included) and `lollo-browser` at 512 MiB, per stack; these are caps, not reservations. At most 4 tasks are open at once per stack, with 8 tabs each. Every page the browser loads goes through the stack's pipelock: often around a hundred requests per page, which count against pipelock's limit of 1200 a minute per site (shared with letta-code's model calls) and each add a receipt of about 1.5 KB, never pruned ([egress-firewall.md](egress-firewall.md#reading-the-receipts)).
- **Adding a stack.** Every stack's browser and pipelock refuse the addresses of every stack on the server. A stack you add reaches the others' lists at their next `lolloctl up`, which recreates their `lollo-pipelock`, `lollo-browser-engine` and `lollo-browser` containers and so ends their open browser tasks; their vaults stay as they are.
- **Data.** Kept sign-ins, per-site settings and the history live on the volume `lollo-<name>_browser-data`, which only `lollo-browser` mounts. Hand-offs and open tasks are only in memory, so a restart ends them. The engine keeps nothing: it mounts no volume.
- **Backups leave it out, on purpose.** The volume holds live sign-ins, and a backup would copy them wherever backups go. `lolloctl backup` skips it, and `restore` leaves it as it is. After a restore on another host, or once the volume is gone, people sign in to those sites again and set **Sites** up again. Notifications are not on this volume: the devices people enabled are kept by the notifications service, whose volume is backed up.
- **Removal.** `lolloctl remove <name>` deletes the volume with the stack, also after the profile was turned off, and says so before asking for confirmation.
- **Logs:** `lolloctl logs <name> lollo-browser` and `lolloctl logs <name> lollo-browser-engine`. The pages agents opened are in the person's **History**, and every request in pipelock's receipts (`lolloctl receipts <name>`).

## Security model
An agent with a browser can sign in and spend money, on pages that may be written to mislead it. What the browser protects, and what it does not.

- **Pages run in a separate container that holds nothing.** Chromium runs in `lollo-browser-engine`, as a non-root user, with every capability dropped and its own sandbox always on: a page that exploits a bug in Chromium still has to escape the sandbox, and then owns a container with no files to steal and nothing it can control. The settings, the history and the kept sign-ins are in the other container, which ignores every request coming from the engine. What a full compromise of the engine does get: the sign-ins of the tasks open at that moment, and what you type during a take-over.
- **The engine's only way out is pipelock.** It is on a network of its own with no route out of the host, so every request a page makes goes through the stack's pipelock, which refuses private, loopback, link-local and cloud-metadata addresses. A page can't reach your vault, Lettuce, the agents or your local network. For this one container, pipelock is a boundary; for everything else in the stack it stays advisory ([The browser's engine](egress-firewall.md#the-browsers-engine)). It checks only where a request goes: it does not see inside HTTPS and lets through any public site.
- **Your own pages are out of reach.** The browser can never open your stacks' Lettuce or lollo pages, nor your Access sign-in page, by any route: a link, a redirect, a frame or a page's script. A task that holds a Google session (you kept `google.com`, signed in with Google during a hand-off, or shared a Google login from the vault) could otherwise sign in to your pages through Access and then act as you there: approve its own vault requests, show hidden fields, allow checkouts. Pipelock refuses those addresses, and Chromium blocks them too; `lolloctl check` refuses a stack where either is missing. Agents never need these pages. Any other application behind the same Cloudflare Access team is out of reach for the browser too. Only those exact addresses are blocked, so other sites on the same domain still work, and a site you keep signed in never keeps the cookies of those addresses, even when it is their domain.
- **Keeping `google.com` signed in is asked twice.** It signs every agent in to every site that offers "Sign in with Google", so **Sites** warns you and keeps it only once you tick **I understand**.
- **Your server's own public address counts as public.** A page can reach whatever your server serves on its public address, from inside the server, which may get past a cloud firewall that would stop the same request from outside. Don't serve anything on the host's public address that relies on the request coming from elsewhere.
- **Agents have no back doors into the browser.** Their tools are a fixed list: no running script in a page, no cookies, no files, no developer tools, no keyboard shortcuts, and no tool a page offers. Agents cannot change a site's settings, keep a sign-in or allow checkouts; only your page does, behind the lollo's Access sign-in, which the browser checks itself. Its pages open cut off from any other tab, and its forms post only from its own pages.
- **Every task starts signed out**, and shares nothing with other tasks. Chromium stores no passwords, cards or history.
- **A kept sign-in belongs to every agent of your stack.** Agents share one server as root, so any of them can use a site you keep signed in, without a vault step. **Keeping a shop where a card is saved lets agents spend there** without asking you or the vault. Keep sign-ins only where you need them, and not where a card is saved.
- **Masking and the site check are aids, not boundaries.** Snapshots and screenshots mask password fields, card numbers, expiry dates, security codes and one-time codes (also those you type during a take-over), and fields typed from the vault. Values typed from the vault are also cut out of everything sent back to the agent when they are 6 characters or more; shorter ones, such as a security code, are hidden only in their field, so a page that repeats them elsewhere shows them. This keeps a well-behaved agent from leaking them into the conversation. The site check stops a confused agent from typing a password into a look-alike page. Neither stops a misled agent: agents are root in your stack, so one can redeem a vault handle itself, or name another site as the expected one. A page that receives a value you approved has it, and can show it or send it on. Approving in the vault stays the decision that matters: read the purpose, and approve only what fits.
- **Recognising a checkout is a guess.** The browser looks for "Place order"-style buttons in a few languages, card fields and checkout addresses. It can miss a page it doesn't know, and a misled agent can place an order there without a hand-off, so the default hand-off is advisory, not a block. Every checkout it recognises is notified and recorded in **History**. What does hold: the vault asks you before an agent gets a card from it, your bank may ask you to confirm a payment (3-D Secure), and the card's own limits. Use a virtual or limited card for anything agents may pay with.
- **Pages can still mislead the agent.** Page text reaches the agent marked as data from a website, never instructions, and the agent is told never to act on what a page asks. That lowers the risk; it does not remove it. Prompt injection is an open problem: the companies building browsing agents report that it is not solved ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/research/prompt-injection-defenses), [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/hardening-atlas-against-prompt-injection)). Page text does not go through pipelock's prompt-injection scan either. A misled agent still has everything an agent has in your stack: a shell, the vault's requests, Gmail if connected. Keep an eye on hand-offs and vault requests that don't fit what you asked for.
- **The live view is protected by your sign-in, not by its password.** The engine's display has an 8-character password, which is weak. What protects it is that only the browser's own network reaches it, and you reach it only through your page, after the Access sign-in and the page's own checks; the password never reaches your device.
- **No backups, on purpose.** Kept sign-ins are never in a backup; see [Running it](#running-it).
- **The admin and Cloudflare.** Someone with root on the server can read the kept sign-ins and watch the browser. Cloudflare sees what you type during a take-over, as it sees all traffic to your stack.
